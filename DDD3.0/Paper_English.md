# Dynamic Diffusion-Driven Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithm via Conditional Diffusion Models

**Abstract**—Large-scale multi-objective optimization problems (LMOPs) pose significant challenges to evolutionary algorithms due to high-dimensional decision variables and exponentially growing search spaces. Traditional multi-objective evolutionary algorithms struggle to effectively explore high-dimensional spaces, while existing problem transformation or decision variable decomposition methods often show limited effectiveness on complex problems. This paper proposes a Dynamic Diffusion-Driven Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithm (DDD-MOEA) based on conditional diffusion models. The algorithm innovatively introduces diffusion models into the field of multi-objective optimization, learning the distribution of high-quality solutions through conditional diffusion models and generating candidate solutions conditioned on target objective values. The algorithm adopts a four-stage framework: GA initial sampling, diffusion model training, guided generation, and hybrid evolution. During the diffusion model training stage, non-dominated solutions from the elite solution archive are used to train the conditional diffusion model, enabling it to learn the complex mapping between decision variables and objective values. In the guided generation stage, target values are dynamically selected based on the current population's needs, and high-quality candidate solutions are generated using the diffusion model. In the hybrid evolution stage, the proportion of offspring generated by the genetic algorithm and diffusion model is adaptively balanced to achieve a trade-off between exploration and exploitation. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm outperforms existing state-of-the-art algorithms on both standard benchmark functions and real-world application problems, particularly showing excellent performance on large-scale problems with 1000-10000 decision variables.

**Index Terms**—Multi-objective optimization, diffusion models, conditional generation, large-scale optimization, evolutionary algorithms.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Research Background

In many real-world engineering and scientific problems, it is often necessary to optimize multiple conflicting objectives simultaneously. Such problems are called Multi-objective Optimization Problems (MOPs). As problem complexity increases, the number of decision variables may reach thousands or even tens of thousands, forming Large-scale Multi-objective Optimization Problems (LMOPs). LMOPs are widely found in machine learning, engineering design, financial investment, network optimization, and many other fields.

The core challenge of large-scale multi-objective optimization problems lies in the "curse of dimensionality": as the dimension of decision variables increases, the search space grows exponentially, making traditional Multi-objective Evolutionary Algorithms (MOEAs) difficult to find satisfactory solution sets within limited computational resources. Specific challenges include:

1. **Explosion of Search Space**: The increase in decision variable dimension D leads to a search space size of O(k^D), where k is the value range of each variable.
2. **High Evaluation Cost**: Objective function evaluations for many practical problems require substantial computational resources, limiting the number of evaluations that can be used.
3. **Solution Sparsity**: In high-dimensional spaces, Pareto optimal solutions often occupy only a very small portion of the search space.
4. **Complex Variable Interactions**: Complex nonlinear interactions may exist between decision variables.

### 1.2 Related Work

To address large-scale multi-objective optimization problems, researchers have proposed various solutions, mainly divided into the following categories:

**Decision Variable Decomposition-based Methods**: These methods divide decision variables into several groups and optimize them separately. Representative algorithms include:
- **CCGDE3**: Adopts a cooperative co-evolution framework, grouping variables and evolving them separately.
- **MOEA/DVA**: Classifies variables based on their contribution to convergence and diversity, optimizing them in stages.
- **LMEA**: Uses variable clustering to handle convergence-related and diversity-related variables separately.

**Problem Transformation-based Methods**: These methods transform the original problem into a smaller-scale problem. Representative algorithms include:
- **WOF**: Uses weight vectors to transform the decision space, reducing optimization variables.
- **LSMOF**: Searches along reference directions, converting the problem into low-dimensional optimization.

**Subspace Learning-based Methods**: These methods learn the Pareto optimal subspace and search in the reduced space. Representative algorithms include:
- **MOEA/PSL**: Uses Restricted Boltzmann Machines and Denoising Autoencoders to learn sparse subspaces.
- **MaOEA-IT**: Adopts PCA to learn the Pareto optimal subspace.

**Surrogate Model-based Methods**: Use computationally inexpensive surrogate models to approximate expensive objective function evaluations. Representative algorithms include:
- **K-RVEA**: Uses Kriging models as surrogates.
- **CSEA**: Uses classifiers to screen promising solutions for evaluation.

### 1.3 Introduction to Diffusion Models

Diffusion Models have achieved breakthrough progress in the field of generative artificial intelligence in recent years. The core idea is to gradually add noise to transform the data distribution into a simple distribution (such as Gaussian distribution), and then learn the reverse process to recover data from noise.

The main advantages of diffusion models include:
1. **Powerful Generation Capability**: Able to generate high-quality, diverse samples.
2. **Stable Training Process**: Unlike GANs, they do not suffer from mode collapse.
3. **Flexible Conditional Control**: Can guide the generation process through conditional information.
4. **Solid Mathematical Foundation**: Have complete probability theory support.

Typical diffusion models include:
- **DDPM** (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models): The foundational diffusion model.
- **DDIM** (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models): An accelerated sampling version.
- **Stable Diffusion**: Latent space diffusion model for image generation.

### 1.4 Research Motivation and Contributions

Although existing methods have made some progress in large-scale multi-objective optimization, they still have the following limitations:

1. **Limited Generation Capability**: Traditional methods rely on simple genetic operators, making it difficult to learn complex high-quality solution distributions.
2. **Insufficient Conditional Control**: Lack the ability to generate corresponding solutions based on specific objective values.
3. **Weak Adaptability**: Difficult to dynamically adjust strategies according to the optimization process.

Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes introducing diffusion models into multi-objective evolutionary algorithms. The main contributions include:

1. **First Application of Conditional Diffusion Models to Multi-objective Optimization**: Innovatively uses conditional diffusion models to learn the distribution of Pareto optimal solutions, achieving candidate solution generation conditioned on objective values.

2. **Proposed Four-Stage Dynamic Driving Framework**: Designs a complete process of GA initial sampling, diffusion model training, guided generation, and hybrid evolution, achieving complementary advantages of two optimization methods.

3. **Designed Adaptive Scheduling Strategy**: Dynamically adjusts the contribution ratio of GA and DM based on the survival rate of diffusion model offspring and optimization stagnation.

4. **Implemented Elite Solution Archive Mechanism**: Manages elite solutions based on hypervolume contribution, providing high-quality training data for the diffusion model.

## 2. Preliminaries

### 2.1 Multi-objective Optimization Problems

A multi-objective optimization problem can be formally defined as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \min \quad \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = (f_1(\mathbf{x}), f_2(\mathbf{x}), \ldots, f_M(\mathbf{x})) \\
& \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{x} \in \Omega
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_D)$ is a D-dimensional decision vector, $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^D$ is the decision space, and $\mathbf{F}: \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^M$ is a vector containing M objective functions.

**Pareto Dominance**: For two solutions $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$, if they satisfy:
- For all $i \in \{1, \ldots, M\}$, $f_i(\mathbf{x}) \leq f_i(\mathbf{y})$
- There exists at least one $j \in \{1, \ldots, M\}$ such that $f_j(\mathbf{x}) < f_j(\mathbf{y})$

Then $\mathbf{x}$ is said to Pareto dominate $\mathbf{y}$, denoted as $\mathbf{x} \prec \mathbf{y}$.

**Pareto Optimal Solution**: If no solution can dominate $\mathbf{x}^*$, then $\mathbf{x}^*$ is called a Pareto optimal solution. The set of all Pareto optimal solutions is called the Pareto Set (PS), and its mapping in the objective space is called the Pareto Front (PF).

### 2.2 Fundamentals of Diffusion Models

#### 2.2.1 Forward Diffusion Process

The forward process of diffusion models gradually adds Gaussian noise to data, defined as a Markov chain:

$$
q(\mathbf{x}_t | \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}, \beta_t\mathbf{I})$$

where $\beta_t \in (0, 1)$ is the noise schedule parameter controlling the amount of noise added at each step.

Through the reparameterization trick, any state at time $t$ can be directly sampled from $\mathbf{x}_0$:

$$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \quad \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$$

where $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$, $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s$.

#### 2.2.2 Reverse Denoising Process

The reverse process of diffusion models starts from pure noise and gradually denoises to recover data:

$$p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} | \mathbf{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t), \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t))$$

Typically, the variance uses a fixed value, and only the mean function is learned. A neural network $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$ is trained to predict the noise:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0, t, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \left[ \|\boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\|^2 \right]$$

#### 2.2.3 Conditional Diffusion Models

Conditional diffusion models introduce additional conditional information $\mathbf{c}$ (such as target vectors) during the generation process:

$$p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} | \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{c}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{c}), \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \mathbf{c}))$$

Conditional information can be injected in various ways:
- **Concatenation**: Concatenate the conditional vector with time step embedding as network input
- **FiLM**: Modulate feature maps using conditional information
- **Cross-attention**: Use attention mechanisms to fuse conditional information

### 2.3 Hypervolume Indicator

Hypervolume (HV) is an important metric for evaluating the performance of multi-objective optimization algorithms, defined as the hypervolume enclosed by the Pareto approximation set and the reference point:

$$HV(\mathcal{P}, \mathbf{r}) = \Lambda\left(\bigcup_{\mathbf{p} \in \mathcal{P}} \{\mathbf{x} | \mathbf{p} \prec \mathbf{x} \prec \mathbf{r}\}\right)$$

where $\mathcal{P}$ is the Pareto approximation set, $\mathbf{r}$ is the reference point, and $\Lambda(\cdot)$ denotes the Lebesgue measure.

The advantages of hypervolume include:
- Simultaneously considers convergence and diversity
- Is a Pareto-compliant metric
- Does not require knowledge of the true Pareto front

## 3. Proposed Algorithm

### 3.1 Algorithm Framework

The proposed Dynamic Diffusion-Driven Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithm (DDD-MOEA) adopts a four-stage framework. The algorithm flow is shown in Algorithm 1.

**Algorithm 1: DDD-MOEA Framework**

```
Input: N (population size), MaxFEs (maximum number of evaluations)
Output: P (final population)

1:  P ← Initialization(N)
2:  Archive ← SolutionArchive(archive_size)
3:  Scheduler ← AdaptiveScheduler(dm_ratio)
4:  
5:  // STAGE 1: GA initial sampling
6:  SamplePop ← GA_Initial_Sampling(Problem, ga_generations, sample_size)
7:  Archive.add(SamplePop)
8:  
9:  // STAGE 2: Train diffusion model
10: DMModel ← ConditionalDiffusionModel(D, M, network_structure)
11: DMModel.train(Archive.getTrainingData(), dm_epochs)
12: 
13: // STAGE 3: Generate initial solutions
14: if DMModel.IsTrained then
15:     TargetObjs ← select_target_objectives(P, N)
16:     P ← DMModel.sample(TargetObjs)
17: else
18:     P ← Problem.Initialization()
19: end if
20: Archive.add(P)
21: 
22: // STAGE 4: Main optimization loop
23: while NotTerminated(P) do
24:     // Generate GA offspring
25:     GAOffspring ← Generate_GA_Offspring(P)
26:     
27:     // Generate DM offspring
28:     dm_count ← Scheduler.getDMOffspringCount(N, DMStats)
29:     if dm_count > 0 then
30:         TargetObjs ← select_target_objectives(P, dm_count)
31:         DMOffspring ← DMModel.sample(TargetObjs)
32:     end if
33:     
34:     // Environmental selection
35:     Combined ← [P, GAOffspring, DMOffspring]
36:     P ← Environmental_Selection(Combined, N)
37:     
38:     // Update archive and statistics
39:     Archive.add([GAOffspring, DMOffspring])
40:     Update_DM_Stats(DMOffspring, P)
41:     
42:     // Update diffusion model
43:     if Should_Update_Model() then
44:         DMModel.train(Archive.getTrainingData(), dm_epochs)
45:     end if
46: end while
47: 
48: return P
```

### 3.2 Conditional Diffusion Model

#### 3.2.1 Model Architecture

The conditional diffusion model uses a feedforward neural network to predict noise. The network structure is shown in Figure 1.

**Input Layer**: Receives concatenation of three inputs:
- Noisy decision variables $\mathbf{x}_t \in \mathbb{R}^D$
- Conditional objective values $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^M$
- Normalized time step $t/T \in \mathbb{R}$

**Hidden Layers**: Uses multiple fully connected layers, default structure is [256, 512, 512, 256].

**Output Layer**: Predicts noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta \in \mathbb{R}^D$.

#### 3.2.2 Training Process

Training data is obtained from the elite solution archive, including decision variable and objective value pairs $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{f})$. The training process is shown in Algorithm 2.

**Algorithm 2: Diffusion Model Training**

```
Input: TrainingData (training data), epochs (training epochs)
Output: Trained model

1:  // Data normalization
2:  X_norm ← normalize(TrainingData.decs)
3:  F_norm ← normalize(TrainingData.objs)
4:  
5:  // Prepare training samples
6:  for each (x, f) in (X_norm, F_norm) do
7:      for j = 1 to 5 do  // Generate 5 noisy versions per sample
8:          t ← random(1, T)
9:          ε ← N(0, I)
10:         x_t ← √ᾱ_t · x + √(1-ᾱ_t) · ε
11:         Add (x_t, f, t/T, ε) to training set
12:     end for
13: end for
14: 
15: // Train network
16: for epoch = 1 to epochs do
17:     for each batch in training_set do
18:         ε_pred ← Network([x_t, f, t/T])
19:         loss ← MSE(ε_pred, ε)
20:         Backpropagate to update network parameters
21:     end for
22: end for
```

#### 3.2.3 Sampling Process

Sampling uses the DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) algorithm for deterministic sampling. The sampling process is shown in Algorithm 3.

**Algorithm 3: DDIM Sampling**

```
Input: TargetObjs (target values), n_samples (number of samples)
Output: GeneratedSolutions (generated solutions)

1:  // Normalize target values
2:  F_norm ← normalize(TargetObjs)
3:  
4:  // Start from random noise
5:  x_T ← N(0, I)
6:  
7:  // Reverse denoising
8:  timesteps ← linspace(T, 1, dm_steps)
9:  for i = 1 to length(timesteps) do
10:     t ← timesteps[i]
11:     ε_θ ← Network([x_t, F_norm, t/T])
12:     
13:     // DDIM update
14:     if i < length(timesteps) then
15:         t_next ← timesteps[i+1]
16:         α_t ← ᾱ[t]
17:         α_next ← ᾱ[t_next]
18:         x_0 ← (x_t - √(1-α_t)·ε_θ) / √α_t
19:         x_next ← √α_next·x_0 + √(1-α_next)·ε_θ
20:     else
21:         // Final step directly predict x_0
22:         x_next ← (x_t - √(1-ᾱ[t])·ε_θ) / √ᾱ[t]
23:     end if
24: end for
25: 
26: // Denormalize
27: GeneratedSolutions ← denormalize(x_0)
28: 
29: return GeneratedSolutions
```

### 3.3 Adaptive Scheduling Strategy

The adaptive scheduling strategy dynamically adjusts the contribution ratio of GA and DM based on the survival rate of diffusion model offspring and optimization stagnation.

#### 3.3.1 DM Offspring Survival Rate Calculation

The DM offspring survival rate is defined as the ratio of DM offspring entering the next generation to the total number of DM offspring:

$$SR_{DM} = \frac{|\{\mathbf{x} \in \text{DMOffspring} \cap \text{NextGen}\}|}{|\text{DMOffspring}|}$$

#### 3.3.2 Dynamic Ratio Adjustment

The DM offspring ratio is dynamically adjusted based on the survival rate:

$$\rho_{t+1} = \begin{cases}
\rho_t \times 1.2 & \text{if } SR_{DM} > 0.3 \\
\rho_t \times 0.8 & \text{if } SR_{DM} < 0.1 \\
\rho_t & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}$$

Meanwhile, the ratio is constrained within a reasonable range: $\rho \in [\rho_{min}, \rho_{max}]$.

### 3.4 Elite Solution Archive

The elite solution archive manages high-quality solutions based on hypervolume contribution, providing training data for the diffusion model.

#### 3.4.1 Hypervolume Contribution Calculation

The hypervolume contribution of each solution is defined as the difference in hypervolume with and without that solution:

$$HV_{contrib}(\mathbf{x}) = HV(\mathcal{P}) - HV(\mathcal{P} \setminus \{\mathbf{x}\})$$

#### 3.4.2 Archive Maintenance

When the archive size exceeds the limit, solutions with the largest hypervolume contributions are retained:

$$\text{Archive} = \{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{P} | HV_{contrib}(\mathbf{x}) \text{ top } K\}$$

### 3.5 Target Value Selection Strategy

The target value selection strategy determines the target direction for solution generation by the diffusion model.

#### 3.5.1 Clustering-based Target Selection

Use k-means clustering to select diverse target values from the current population and archive:

1. Merge target values from population and archive
2. Normalize target values
3. Use k-means clustering into n_clusters clusters
4. Use cluster centers as target values

#### 3.5.2 Exploration-Exploitation Balance

Balance exploration and exploitation in target selection:
- **Exploitation**: Select regions where the current population performs well
- **Exploration**: Select sparse regions in the archive

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Advantages of Diffusion Models

Experimental results show that diffusion models have the following advantages in multi-objective optimization:

1. **Powerful Generation Capability**: Able to learn complex high-quality solution distributions and generate diverse candidate solutions.
2. **Precise Conditional Control**: Can generate corresponding solutions based on target values, improving search efficiency.
3. **Stable Training Process**: Training is more stable compared to GANs and other generative models.

### 5.2 Limitations

Although DDD-MOEA has achieved excellent performance, it still has the following limitations:

1. **High Computational Cost**: Training and sampling of diffusion models require substantial computational resources.
2. **Parameter Sensitivity**: Network architecture, noise schedule, and other parameters have significant impact on performance.
3. **High Data Requirements**: Sufficient training data is needed to leverage the advantages of diffusion models.

### 5.3 Future Work

Future research directions include:

1. **Lightweight Diffusion Models**: Develop more computationally efficient diffusion model architectures.
2. **Adaptive Parameter Adjustment**: Implement online parameter learning and adjustment.
3. **Multi-model Ensemble**: Use multiple diffusion models to learn distributions of different regions separately.
4. **Extension to Many-objective**: Study the application of diffusion models in many-objective optimization.

This paper proposes a Dynamic Diffusion-Driven Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithm (DDD-MOEA) based on conditional diffusion models. The algorithm innovatively introduces diffusion models into the field of multi-objective optimization, learning the distribution of high-quality solutions through conditional diffusion models and generating candidate solutions conditioned on target objective values. The algorithm adopts a four-stage framework, achieving complementary advantages of genetic algorithms and diffusion models.

Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm outperforms existing state-of-the-art algorithms on both standard benchmark functions and real-world application problems, particularly showing excellent performance on large-scale and sparse problems. Ablation studies verify the effectiveness of each component.

Despite some limitations, the application of diffusion models in multi-objective optimization has broad prospects. Future research will further optimize the algorithm, improve computational efficiency, and extend to more application scenarios.
